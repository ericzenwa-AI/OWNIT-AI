Inside ownIT v2

A tutor sends the A-level maths question a student is stuck on. The system works downwards through what that question rests on, one question at a time, until it finds the thing that is actually missing.

108 skills 49 doorways 10 topics 550 questions 308 tests

Deployed and running at ownit-tzsv.onrender.com

The one-sentence version

Say this out loud and most people get it: “A student who can’t do a differentiation question usually isn’t missing differentiation. This finds what they’re actually missing.”

Every exam question sits on top of other skills, which sit on top of other skills. A student stuck at the top could be broken anywhere below. Asking them “what do you not understand?” doesn’t work, because if they knew, they wouldn’t be stuck. So instead it tests downwards until something fails, then keeps going under that failure until it reaches solid ground.

It is written to tutors, not students. That decision drives the whole front end: a tutor is the one who can act on the answer, and it avoids collecting children’s email addresses.

What happens, end to end

  1. A question arrivesentry.py

    Typed, or a photo, or a PDF. A photo is better — copying maths into text destroys it, and there is proof: (1 − 9x)^½ arrived as 19 1 2 x when pasted, and read perfectly from a picture. The model reads it and matches it to one of the 49 doorways. If it can’t, it says so rather than guessing.

  2. If it has parts, it asks which oneapi.py

    A past paper question is usually (a) to (d). It reads all of them, shows all of them, and asks which one the student is stuck on. Part (a) is not assumed — that is normally the part they could do. Parts the map cannot reach are shown too, and filed as coverage gaps.

  3. The walkwalk.py

    The diagnostic itself, covered below. A tutor watching sees the descent build up a question at a time rather than only at the end.

  4. The reportapi.py · index.html

    Leads with “Teach this first” — one skill. Then why that one, then what was not checked and an offer to look.

  5. Was it right?store.py

    A tutor marks the diagnosis right or wrong, names the real gap, and adds a note. This is the only signal that can correct the map — everything else measures how the walk behaved, not whether it was correct.

The map everything rests on

One file, data/skills.yaml, holds 108 skills. Nothing else in the system knows any maths — it all comes from here.

- id: first_principles
  name: Differentiation from first principles
  level: 1
  topic: differentiation
  kind: procedure
  probe: Derive the derivative of a simple function using the limit definition.
  needs: [limit_h_to_zero, expand_brackets, algebraic_fractions]
  • level — how far from the exam. 0 is what a paper asks; 4 is bedrock like negative numbers.
  • needs — what this rests on. These links are the whole map.
  • kind — changes the shape of the question written about it. 70 are procedure, 23 concept, 15 fact.
  • topic — only on doorways. This is the subtle one.
Why only 49 of the 108 carry a topic

Index laws show up in differentiation, in logs, in surds, in binomials. If it carried a topic it would be a doorway, and a question could get matched to it — starting the diagnosis halfway down the ladder. So shared skills carry none. Having one means “an exam question can legitimately start here”, and that is the entire difference between the 49 doorways and the other 59 skills.

On load, graph.py refuses to start if any needs points at a skill that doesn’t exist, if the links form a loop, or if nothing has a topic. A broken map fails at startup rather than halfway through a session.

The walk

Start at the doorway. Ask about one thing it rests on. If they can do it, try the next. If they can’t, go underneath that failure. Repeat. When something fails and everything beneath it holds, that is the answer.

The rules, and what each is for

  • Closest first. Siblings are asked nearest-to-the-question first, so the questions feel connected to what they came for.
  • Five levels deep. Was two. Measuring it changed that — numbers below.
  • Fifteen questions maximum. Rarely reached; the average is 5.9.
  • Three “I don’t know”s in a row stops it. Grinding to bedrock helps nobody.
  • “I don’t know” is always offered, always last. It counts as not-held so the walk descends, but it is stored separately from a wrong answer. Those are different students.
  • Skipped branches are recorded. Going down one branch means not going down others. The report names them.

Be precise about what is real here

The walk, the order, the verdicts and the question count are genuine output of the real diagnostic against the real map — re-run to confirm it still matches. The student is simulated from the examiner report, not a person who sat down and answered. “A real captured run” would overstate it.

The question bank, and why it was rebuilt

Questions are written once per skill and served to many students, which makes a session nearly free. 550 live questions cover all 108 skills; 77 are retired.

The important part is not the count. It is that every one has been checked by a model that did not write it, and that checking found a lot.

How the check works

The checker is never told which option is marked correct. It gets the question and all four options and has to work it out and pick one. Only then is its pick compared with the stored key. Showing it the key would measure how agreeable it is, and comparing free-text answers would drown in false alarms — 15x^4 - 4x^(-3) and 15x^4 - 4/x^3 are the same answer written twice. Picking a letter has neither problem. Anything the two disagree on goes to a stronger model before being retired.

The harder the arithmetic, the more often the answer key was wrong. Expanding brackets and negative numbers were almost always right; areas between curves were wrong a third of the time.

Why that mattered more than it sounds

A wrong answer key is the worst failure this system has, and eval.py cannot see it — eval tests the walk’s reasoning and takes the questions on trust. A student who answers correctly gets recorded as not holding the skill, and the descent goes hunting a gap that is not there. The report looks completely normal.

The fix, measured rather than assumed

Generation moved from Haiku to Sonnet. The comparison that justified it, run on the five worst-hit skills, four questions each, checked blind:

45%Haiku — 9 of 20 answer keys wrong
0%Sonnet — 0 of 20 wrong

Then the 71 replacements Haiku had already written were audited: 19 wrong, 27%. Rewritten on Sonnet, and all 22 rewrites passed on the first check.

The bank travels as data/question_bank.jsonl, committed to the repository, one question per line. That is what stops any of this being paid for twice — and what fills an empty database at startup, which matters because the host wipes the filesystem on every deploy.

A session costs one model call — reading the question. An attempt adds two more, and those now run side by side rather than one after the other, which took the wait from about 17 seconds to about 11. The questions themselves come off the shelf and cost nothing.

What is actually measured

eval.py invents students. It hides one skill, breaks everything resting on it, runs the walk, and checks whether the hidden skill comes back. Every skill under every doorway — 718 runs.

87%gaps within reach, named correctly
0false positives in 718 runs
5.9questions on average, 13 at worst
100%out-of-reach gaps left unconfirmed

Zero false positives is the one that matters. Missing a gap wastes a session; naming a skill the student already has sends a tutor to teach the wrong thing and stops anyone looking further. It is the failure that would make this actively harmful, and it has never happened in testing.

Two other measured numbers: 60% of questions on a real Edexcel Pure paper can be placed at a doorway (up from 20% before five topics were added), and a diagnosis costs about $0.22.

Running it for real

  • Deployed on Render from render.yaml, with a persistent disk. Without one the filesystem goes on every deploy and every restart, and SQLite goes with it — waitlist signups, tutor verdicts, coverage gaps. The bank would survive, being committed; nothing else would.
  • Health answers two questions from outside. storage: kept means the disk mounted; questions: 550 means the shelf restocked. A disk that failed to mount looks exactly like a working app until somebody notices the waitlist is empty.
  • A daily ceiling on questions read, because the page is public with no sign-in and every reading is a call to the best model. It counts readings rather than walks, since a reading is the thing that costs money.
  • Maths is typeset. The bank stores x^(-2) and sqrt(136) because that is what the model writes; it is converted to LaTeX at the moment of display.
  • Feedback is readable at /admin/feedback, password-protected, which refuses to open at all when no password is set.

The questions people will ask you

Why multiple choice? Isn’t that weak?

Because each wrong option is a named mistake. A student picking option C doesn’t just tell us they’re wrong — it tells us they subtracted the indices instead of adding them. Free text would need marking, which means another model call, more cost, more failure.

Why five levels deep and not two?

Two was the original call, on the grounds that telling an A-level student they are missing Year 7 arithmetic is true but useless. Measuring it: at two levels the walk finds the real cause 40% of the time, at five it is 87%, and the cost is about three extra questions for the students who genuinely have deep gaps.

The reasoning had been wrong in a specific way — confusing what a tutor should teach with how far we should look. The report shows the whole chain, so a tutor can start wherever they judge best, but only if we bothered to look.

How do you know the diagnosis isn’t confident nonsense?

This is the strongest question anyone can ask. The answer is eval.py: 718 invented students with known gaps, 87% found, zero cases of naming a skill the student could actually do. It is not the same as testing on real students — that has not happened yet — but it is considerably better than a demo and a good feeling.

And separately, audit.py: every question in the bank has been checked by a model that did not write it.

Why doesn’t the student see the prerequisite trail?

A student seeing “your A-level question actually failed at fraction arithmetic” learns something demoralising and not actionable. The tutor needs the chain; the student needs one next step. The same reasoning keeps the running descent tutor-only, and keeps skill names off the question screen.

What has actually gone wrong while building it?

Worth knowing, because the pattern repeats: every one of these passed the whole test suite while being visibly broken.

A comment containing a closing script tag ended the script block early and printed two thousand characters of JavaScript on the page. A model override swapped the model but left an incompatible setting behind. Restoring the bank only ever added, so retiring a wrong question here left it live on the server. And both pages had no cache header, so a browser could run last week’s build indefinitely — which made two working features look broken at once.

Each one produced a test that would now catch it. The pages are parsed as HTML on every run specifically because a JavaScript engine cannot see that class of bug.

What is not done

  • No real students yet. Every accuracy number comes from simulations.
  • 40% of a real paper still cannot be placed. No trigonometry, circles, vectors, proof, numerical methods, statistics or mechanics — and statistics and mechanics are roughly a third of A-level Maths.
  • Tutor mode is a flag, not a login. ?tutor=1 keeps the verdict form and the running descent away from students; it stops nobody who edits a URL.
  • Anyone can call the API. Cross-origin requests are unrestricted. The daily ceiling limits the damage rather than preventing it.
  • Nothing has been read yet. The feedback table, the comments table and the coverage backlog all work and are all empty, because no tutor has used it.
