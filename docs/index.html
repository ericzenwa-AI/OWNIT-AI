What it does

A student sends the question they are stuck on. It finds what is missing underneath it.

Differentiation sits on indices. Indices sit on knowing what a power means. When a student gets a question wrong, the mark scheme blames that topic — so that is the topic they revise, and nothing changes, because the break is two years further down.

ownIT walks down a map of 196 skills, one layer at a time, asking a short question at each, until something gives way. Usually three questions. Then it teaches that one thing, step by step, and brings them back to their own question. It never gives them the answer to it.

It is live, free, and takes about three minutes: ownitmaths.com.

Checking the output

How I know the answer key is right

The questions are written by a model. So is the mark on which option is correct — and nothing checks that. It is the worst thing a diagnostic can get wrong, because it is invisible: the student answers correctly, is recorded as not knowing the skill, and the diagnosis goes hunting for a gap that was never there. The report looks completely normal.

So I had a second model solve every question from scratch, never shown the stored answer. Where the two disagreed, a third decided.

55 of the first 531 had the wrong answer marked correct. Not one of them looked wrong. 77 were retired.

The errors were not spread evenly, and that was the useful part. Expanding brackets and negative numbers came out right nearly every time; areas between curves and binomial expansions were wrong about a third of the time. Writing the question was never the hard part. Working out the answer was.

That one finding changed the architecture. Question generation moved off the cheap model, and the economics turned out to point the same way: a question is written once and served to hundreds of students, so the cost of writing it is spread across all of them, while a wrong answer key is charged to every single one.

Every question added since goes through the same gate. The statistics and probability topics I added most recently were 120 questions; all 120 were confirmed, with one dispute escalated to the stronger model and the stored answer upheld. A test fails the build if any skill in the map has no questions on the shelf.

What it costs

What a session costs, and why the teaching runs on the cheaper model

Every call is assigned a model deliberately, and the rule is not "use the best one". It is what does being wrong here cost?

Reading the student's question    Opus      once per session
Writing a diagnostic question      Sonnet    once per skill, ever
A turn of the lesson               Sonnet    ten times per lesson
Explaining a skill                 Sonnet    once per skill, ever

Reading the question gets the expensive model because it runs once and a misread sends the entire session somewhere untrue — every question after it is about the wrong topic. A teaching turn gets the cheaper one because it runs ten times, it is grounded in an explanation that was already written and checked, and the student is sitting there waiting. Being wrong costs a sentence, not a diagnosis.

1.7p – 4.7p per diagnosis, measured against the real prompts with a token counter rather than estimated. A ten-turn lesson adds about 5p.

The question bank is the other half. Diagnostic questions are written once and stored, so the common path through the app makes no model call at all beyond reading the question — which is why a retake, where the skill is already known, costs nothing.

Knowing the number mattered more than shrinking it. It set the daily ceiling, it decided which model goes where, and when I later costed the same app for Nigerian schools, the question "what will a school pay per student per term, divided by sessions" was answerable in a minute.

When it broke

The three days the app was blank

I redesigned the page that shows a student their result. In the code, the screens are built as JavaScript template literals — text wrapped in backticks. I left explanatory comments inside one, naming the fields the way the rest of the codebase names them: in backticks.

A backtick inside a template ends the template. The rest of the script became a syntax error, nothing on the page ran, and every student who opened the diagnostic got a header and nothing else.

Three days live, and the whole test suite passing throughout.

That is the part worth sitting with. There were hundreds of tests, and they all passed, because every one of them read the page as text and the text was perfectly correct. Nothing ever ran it. A page that is text-correct and script-broken sails through a test suite that only ever reads.

The fix took a minute. What I did afterwards took longer and mattered more:

  • Wrote a test for exactly this fault, and ran it against the broken version first to prove it fails — a test you have never seen fail is a test you are trusting on faith.
  • Added a second, cruder net: every template that opens must close.
  • Started loading the real pages in a headless browser after deploying, rather than checking that the new code had merely arrived.

It was found by a user, not by me, and the user was the person I built it for. I had checked that the deploy landed. I had not checked that the page worked.

How I work

Things I decided not to build

Most of what I can show is in the repository — but the decisions I am most pleased with are removals.

  • A feature that asked students to explain their wrong answers. Built, shipped, then deleted a week later: it never changed the diagnosis, and nothing downstream read what it stored. It was a nicer moment, not a better answer.
  • Generated diagrams. Four rendering faults had already reached real students. A wrong diagram is worse than a wrong answer key, because it looks authoritative and nobody re-reads it. Diagrams in this project are drawn by hand and committed, which is also why whole topics are honestly marked "not covered" rather than faked.
  • A paywall, two days after building it, when the experiment it was meant to run stopped being the right one.

The same instinct runs through the measurement. I ran a check on 196 student-facing explanations and 85 of them failed — some of them genuinely wrong, not merely clumsy. That number is in the repository and on this page because the alternative is not knowing it.

Where I am going

What I am looking for

Work where the hard part is knowing whether the system is right, not getting it to produce something. I came to this from teaching rather than from software, which shows in both directions: I am newer to the code than most, and more careful than most about what the output is actually doing to the person reading it.
